How is feta cheese made?
:::

:::ai
Feta is made by **curdling milk, draining the curds, salting them, and aging them in brine**.

Traditionally, Greek feta is made mostly from **sheep’s milk**, sometimes blended with **goat’s milk**. The basic process is:

1. **Warm and culture the milk.** Cheesemaking bacteria are added to slightly acidify the milk.
2. **Add rennet.** This coagulates the milk into a soft gel-like curd.
3. **Cut the curd.** Cutting releases whey, the watery portion of the milk.
4. **Drain it.** The curds are transferred into molds or cloth and allowed to drain and firm up. Unlike many hard cheeses, feta generally isn’t heavily pressed.
5. **Salt the cheese.** The blocks are salted, which draws out additional moisture and helps develop the characteristic flavor and texture.
6. **Age it in brine.** The feta sits submerged in salty water for weeks or months. This is what gives it that **salty, tangy flavor and moist, crumbly texture**.

One interesting detail: the tanginess isn't just from the salt—the bacterial cultures convert lactose into **lactic acid**, giving feta its sour brightness. The brine then preserves it and continues shaping the flavor.

Authentic Greek **PDO feta** must be made in designated areas of Greece from sheep’s milk, with up to 30% goat’s milk permitted. Cow’s-milk cheeses sold as “feta” elsewhere can taste noticeably milder and have a different texture.

:::ai
Yes — people have tried it. The interesting wrinkle is that **human milk is actually rather bad cheesemaking milk**.

Human milk has much less protein than cow, sheep, or goat milk, and a smaller proportion of that protein is casein, the protein that forms the curd when rennet is added. Dairy-science experiments have found that human milk by itself doesn't form a firm conventional cheese curd very well. [WIRED](https://www.wired.com/story/science-of-cheese/) So some famous “human milk cheeses” have actually been blends. In 2010, New York chef Daniel Angerer experimented with surplus milk produced by his wife; at least one documented version of his recipe combined human milk with ordinary milk so that it would coagulate properly. [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2010/mar/09/breast-milk-cheese) Artist Miriam Simun also made **Human Cheese** in 2011 as an art/food project, deliberately using the visceral reaction to it to explore ideas about bodies, food systems, animals, and culture. [Esse](https://esse.ca/en/portfolios/miriam-simun/)

And I think the second part of your question is much more interesting.

Biologically, you're right: **human lactation isn't some categorically different phenomenon from cow or goat lactation.** We're mammals. Mammary glands produce a nutrient-rich secretion to feed offspring. From a strictly zoological point of view, “human milk” is no stranger a substance than “goat milk.”

Yet the phrase **“human-milk cheese” produces a completely different psychological response**.

Sexualization is part of that, but I think several overlapping cultural boundaries are operating at once.

First, we have a very strong **human/animal boundary** around food. We comfortably categorize a cow as a source of milk, muscle tissue, hide, etc. We don't ordinarily categorize humans as sources of edible biological products. So the moment you apply familiar agricultural language — _milk yield, cheese, meat, harvesting_ — to humans, it suddenly sounds disturbing. The biological continuity becomes unusually visible.

Second, human milk is culturally understood as **relational rather than agricultural**. We picture a mother nursing her infant: an intimate interaction between two particular people. Cow's milk has mostly been abstracted away from that relationship. It arrives as an anonymous white liquid in a carton. We don't generally imagine the cow, her mammary gland, her recent pregnancy, her calf, or somebody extracting her milk. “Human milk cheese” forces all of that embodiment back into consciousness.

That's why there's an interesting asymmetry:

> “Sheep's-milk feta” sounds gastronomic.  
> “Human-milk feta” sounds bodily.

Yet both descriptions identify the species whose mammary secretion you're eating.

And yes, breasts occupy an unusually complicated cultural category because they are simultaneously **sexual organs in our social imagination and feeding organs biologically**. In many contemporary cultures, especially American culture, the sexual meaning is so dominant that lactation can itself acquire an awkward sexual adjacency even when nothing sexual is actually occurring. Angerer himself remarked on essentially this phenomenon: human bodily fluids immediately acquire an intimate or sexual connotation in a way animal milk doesn't. [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2010/mar/09/breast-milk-cheese)

But I don't think sexualization explains all of the “ick.” Consider that **human hair in food, human blood, human saliva, or even the idea of eating human placenta** can provoke a similar reaction without necessarily being sexual. There seems to be a broader psychological category of **“substances from another person's body.”** Once something crosses the body's boundary, we become highly attentive to contamination, identity, consent, and intimacy.

There may even be good evolutionary reasons for some of that disgust response: bodily fluids can transmit pathogens. But culture determines tremendously **which bodily substances we mentally exempt from that reaction**. Cow's milk has been culturally transformed from “a bodily fluid from a lactating animal” into simply “food.”

And that last point gets especially strange when you dwell on it. We routinely drink the milk of **another species**, generally intended biologically for its young, while the milk our own species evolved specifically for human nutrition strikes many adults as grotesque.

That's not an argument that we _should_ be eating human-milk cheese. It's just a fascinating demonstration that **“normal food” isn't a biological category. It's a cultural one.**

In fact, that's basically why projects like Simun's are interesting. The cheese itself is almost beside the point. It exposes a conceptual switch in your head: _animal → food; human → body_. And once you notice that switch, ordinary dairy becomes perceptually strange for a moment too. [foodtechconnect.com](https://foodtechconnect.com/2011/03/08/human-cheese-questions-answered/)

:::ai
Yes. For many **ethical vegans**, one important shift is precisely from seeing _milk as a substance_ to seeing **the reproductive relationship that produces the substance**.

A carton of milk is extraordinarily good at concealing its origins. It presents milk almost as though cows simply _make milk_, in the way an apple tree makes apples. But mammalian lactation is part of reproduction. A dairy cow begins a lactation after giving birth; commercial dairy management then typically aims for another pregnancy and roughly annual calving so that milk production and herd replacement continue. [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/is-calving-stressful-in-dairy-cows)

So if you mentally reconstruct the process behind the carton, it becomes something more like:

**female animal → pregnancy → birth → maternal lactation → calf → milk extraction → human consumption**

rather than simply:

**cow → milk.**

That first representation is much closer to what many vegans are responding to.

### The calf makes the ethical picture especially different

In conventional dairying, the calf generally doesn't simply stay with its mother and drink whatever it wants while humans take the excess. Early cow-calf separation is common; scientific reviews describe separation within hours or days as standard practice, although practices vary and cow-calf-contact dairies do exist. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31103300/) USDA dairy-management material likewise describes newborn calves being removed promptly after birth and the cow then entering the lactating herd. [APHIS](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/sites/default/files/dairy_industry_manual.pdf)

There are **real husbandry reasons** offered for early separation besides maximizing saleable milk: monitoring colostrum intake, disease control, and questions about whether later separation after a stronger bond produces greater acute distress. The welfare literature doesn't reduce this to a simple good/bad proposition; extended contact permits maternal and social behaviors, while different systems involve different health and management tradeoffs. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31103300/)

But an ethical vegan may say that those details don't resolve the more fundamental issue. The system exists because humans want the mother's milk. Her reproductive system, pregnancy, offspring, and lactation therefore become parts of a **production process**.

And this is where your human-milk thought experiment becomes surprisingly illuminating.

Imagine this sentence:

> “We impregnate the women periodically, remove their babies shortly after birth, collect the milk mechanically, and sell it.”

That sounds horrifying almost before you've specified how well anyone is treated.

Now replace _women_ with _cows_ and you've described, in very stripped-down biological language, some central features of dairy production.

Obviously, **humans and cows aren't ethically interchangeable**, and that analogy by itself doesn't establish what our obligations to cattle ought to be. But it exposes something that ordinary dairy vocabulary normally hides. Words like _freshening_, _lactation cycle_, _herd replacement_, _milk yield_, and _calf management_ make a mammalian reproductive process sound industrial and abstract.

That's one reason encountering dairy through vegan writing can produce an almost perceptual change: **you suddenly see breasts, pregnancy and motherhood where previously you saw “dairy.”**

And that connects strongly to what you were noticing about human milk.

### We have culturally “de-bodied” animal milk

Think about how strange the abstraction is.

You pour cream into coffee and probably don't experience it as:

> _a secretion from the mammary gland of a recently pregnant female bovine._

You experience it as **cream**.

The biological origin has disappeared into the food category.

But if somebody put human breast milk in your coffee, it would be almost impossible _not_ to imagine the person. You'd immediately wonder:

- Whose milk is this?
- Why was she lactating?
- Where is her baby?
- Did she choose to give this?
- How was it collected?
- Is this intimate?
- Is this sanitary?
- Is this somehow sexual?

The **person and her body remain psychologically attached to the substance.**

With cow's milk, we've learned to detach them.

That's partly culture, partly industrialization, partly species difference. The carton itself performs a kind of psychological transformation: **mammalian secretion → commodity**.

### And different vegan philosophies object at somewhat different levels

An animal-welfare-oriented vegan might focus primarily on suffering: separation distress, confinement, painful procedures, reproductive demands, illness, transport and slaughter. Someone with that framework could conceivably regard a radically different dairy system with prolonged cow-calf contact and excellent welfare as substantially better.

An animal-rights vegan can make a more fundamental argument: even if the cow were treated extremely well, **her reproductive capacity and milk are being appropriated for somebody else's purposes without anything resembling consent**. The objection isn't merely “factory farming is cruel”; it's that the animal has been made into a means of production.

And some **feminist/eco-feminist vegan thought** focuses particularly on this reproductive dimension: female animals' fertility, pregnancy, lactation and offspring becoming economically productive assets. From that perspective, dairy can be morally more revealing than meat because the commodity literally arises from a female mammal's reproductive biology.

That doesn't mean one has to adopt that conclusion. But I think it's an intellectually serious way of looking at dairy.

And there's a fascinating psychological inversion here: **human breast milk initially made animal milk seem less weird to you; then looking carefully at animal milk can make ordinary dairy seem considerably weirder.**

The “weirdness” hasn't really moved. What's changed is **which parts of the biological reality your cultural lens allows you to see.**

:::ai
Yes. I think you're putting your finger on two different technological/cultural shifts that could meet in a very interesting place: **make the animal more visible in the existing system, while simultaneously developing foods that don't require the animal at all.**

And, remarkably, the second one is already happening.

### We can make genuine dairy proteins without a cow

The technology is called **precision fermentation**. Very roughly, scientists take the genetic instructions for a milk protein—say bovine whey protein or casein—and give those instructions to microorganisms such as yeast or fungi. Feed the microbes sugar in fermentation tanks, and they manufacture the protein. You then separate and purify it.

So instead of:

**grass/feed → cow → pregnancy/lactation → milk → protein**

you get:

**sugar → microorganism → milk protein**

The resulting protein isn't oat protein pretending to be dairy protein. It can be the actual milk protein that gives dairy its characteristic nutritional or functional properties. Perfect Day, for example, has been commercially producing precision-fermented whey protein; because the protein itself is a milk protein, it still has to be treated as a **milk allergen**, despite never having been inside a cow. [Perfect Day](https://perfectday.com/animal-free-milk-protein/)

That distinction is fascinating. A person with lactose intolerance may be fine with such a product because it doesn't necessarily contain lactose. A person with a milk-protein allergy may **not** be fine with it because, at the molecular level, their immune system recognizes the protein as dairy.

And you can reconstruct something milk-like around those proteins using water, fats, sugars, minerals, etc. That's how products such as animal-free dairy beverages have been made. [Perfect Day](https://perfectday.com/newsroom/bored-cow-sprouts/)

### Cheese is the harder—and perhaps more consequential—problem

And this brings us directly back to your feta.

If the goal is really good cheese, **casein is crucial**. It's the protein that forms the network responsible for curd formation, stretch, melt and much of cheese's texture.

Casein turns out to be significantly harder to manufacture by precision fermentation than whey. It doesn't merely need to exist; its phosphorylation and assembly into structures resembling natural **casein micelles** matter enormously for cheesemaking. Two very recent scientific reviews, including one published this month, describe this as one of the central remaining technical challenges. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S092422442600350X)

But people are doing it. Bay Area company New Culture is producing precision-fermented casein and has developed mozzarella from it; as of September 2026 they're still describing the broader restaurant launch as forthcoming, beginning through pizzerias rather than as something sitting throughout supermarket dairy cases. [New Culture](https://www.newculture.com/blog/another-granted-patent-for-new-culture/)

So your imagined solution—

**“I don't want to give up cheese; can we just make the cheese without involving a cow?”**

—is essentially an active field of food biotechnology.

And I suspect cheese may be where this technology becomes really compelling to ordinary omnivores. Plant-based milk is already pretty good. Plant-based cheese is… much more obviously an imitation. If you can produce actual casein without an animal, suddenly you can potentially get **real curd formation, stretch, browning and maturation chemistry** without trying to make coconut oil and starch impersonate mozzarella.

Feta-like cheese is theoretically possible too, although traditional feta's sheep/goat proteins, fat composition, cultures and aging all contribute to its particular character. We're not yet at the supermarket stage where you can choose “traditional sheep feta” and “precision-fermented animal-free feta” side by side.

And I think there's an important psychological reason eggs became an especially effective target.

A **battery cage is extremely legible**.

Show somebody a photograph of chickens packed into cages unable to spread their wings and almost no agricultural education is required. People immediately understand the welfare problem.

Dairy is much harder to encode in one photograph. A modern dairy cow may be standing comfortably in a clean free-stall barn, eating plentiful food. The ethical questions involve an entire **life cycle**—insemination, pregnancy, calving, separation practices, milking, housing, disease, culling—not necessarily one visually shocking environment.

So “cage-free” became an extraordinarily powerful consumer concept.

There's no equally simple dairy label corresponding to it.

Maybe **“calf stays with mother”** would be one.  
Or **“pasture-based.”**  
Or **“cow-calf contact.”**

But those haven't entered American consumer consciousness at anything like the same scale.

And this brings me to the part of your comment I think is most important.

### Industrial food depends enormously on abstraction

Not necessarily because everyone involved is malevolent or concealing something. Large-scale food systems _have_ to compress extraordinary biological complexity into commodities.

A pound of hamburger is standardized.

A gallon of milk is standardized.

A carton of eggs is standardized.

A block of feta is standardized.

And every stage of standardization progressively removes information about **the particular organism from which the food originated**.

Your feta doesn't tell you:

- which sheep or cows produced its milk;
- how they lived;
- how often they gave birth;
- what happened to their offspring;
- how long they stayed in the herd;
- what happened to them afterward.

It says something like:

**FETA — 8 OZ — CULTURED PASTEURIZED MILK, SALT, ENZYMES.**

Biological history collapses into an ingredient list.

And you're probably right that greater transparency would produce a mixture of reactions rather than universal veganism. Some people would stop buying particular things. Some would pay considerably more for different husbandry. Some would decide, _I've looked at this, and I'm okay with it._ And some would deliberately avoid looking.

That last possibility is important. Human beings do this everywhere. We outsource things that are unpleasant to confront directly: slaughter, waste disposal, sewage, elder care, mining, hazardous manufacturing. **Distance makes modern civilization possible, but distance also makes moral disengagement possible.**

Ancient pastoral systems don't automatically solve the ethical question—preindustrial humans could certainly treat animals harshly too. But one major difference is **proximity**. If your household owns six goats, you know the goats. You know which one gave birth. You see the kid. You milk her yourself. You know when she's sick. You know when she dies.

The ethical relationship may or may not be better, but it is much harder for it to become **invisible**.

And that's why I actually find your “I don't want to give up cheese!” position interesting rather than contradictory. There is a potentially enormous constituency of people who aren't trying to become vegan and don't regard using animals for food as inherently wrong, but who would nevertheless say:

**“If I can get genuinely excellent mozzarella or feta without requiring an industrial reproductive system involving millions of animals, why wouldn't I?”**

That may ultimately be a much larger market than ethical veganism.

The really transformative technology may therefore not be something that asks people to develop a new appetite. It may be something that lets them keep the old one while quietly replacing **the biological machinery behind it**.

Yes — I think there’s a coherent thread underneath the scattershot quality. You’re describing **how moral attention itself has a cost**, and therefore how much of everyday ethics gets delegated.

Most of us cannot investigate the causal history of every object we touch. Milk, electricity, smartphones, clothing, coffee, medications, investments: each opens into an enormous supply chain with questions about labor, ecology, animals, politics, safety, and so on. If every grocery purchase required a dissertation, ordinary life would become impossible. So we develop defaults:

> _This is sold at a normal supermarket, therefore somebody has presumably decided it is acceptable enough._

That delegation isn't necessarily stupidity or indifference. It is partly a basic requirement of living inside a complex society. But it creates the opening you're noticing: **whoever defines the default gets enormous moral power.** Regulation does some of that; markets do some; cultural norms do some; packaging and branding do some; journalists, activists, scientists, chefs, celebrities, and increasingly algorithms do some.

And then occasionally something punctures the abstraction. You learn what a battery cage looks like, or where veal comes from, or what happens to calves in some dairy systems, and suddenly a previously effortless object becomes a **decision**. That's psychologically expensive. “Don't bother me with this” is a very understandable response because knowledge has created an obligation to reconcile something.

There’s an interesting asymmetry there:

**ignorance → no felt decision**  
**knowledge → moral friction**

And humans can't indefinitely maximize moral friction.

So one of the most powerful forms of progress is probably not getting everyone to become infinitely conscientious consumers. It's **changing the default so conscientiousness isn't required**.

That is where your “artificial chees-telligence” joke actually points at something profound. 😄 If precision-fermented cheese eventually becomes:

- indistinguishable in taste and cooking behavior,
- nutritionally comparable,
- demonstrably safe,
- competitively priced,
- environmentally cheaper,
- and produced without the animal-welfare problems,

then choosing it stops being a moral achievement.

It can simply become **cheese**.

That is historically how a lot of technological change succeeds. People usually don't want to repeatedly sacrifice something they enjoy in order to instantiate an ethical principle. But they'll readily adopt an alternative that removes the conflict altogether.

And molecular familiarity really does matter psychologically. There's an enormous difference between:

**“Here is an imitation cheese made out of plants.”**

and

**“Here is the same casein molecule, made by fermentation instead of by a cow.”**

The second story preserves continuity. It says: _you're not abandoning cheese; we're changing its manufacturing process._

That still won't eliminate cultural resistance. Food is far more than chemistry. It is ancestry, landscape, ritual, memory, status, craft. A fourth-generation cheesemaker isn't merely operating an inefficient casein-production device. There is a whole way of life there. A traditional sheep's-milk feta from a particular region has relationships among animals, grasses, microbes, climate, people, and technique that aren't captured by saying “we synthesized the protein.”

So I doubt the future is simply **old dairy disappears → fermentation tanks win**.

More plausibly, there could eventually be a bifurcation:

**commodity dairy** becomes increasingly fermentation-derived because there's little reason to involve millions of animals just to produce standardized mozzarella, whey powder, milk proteins, etc.

Meanwhile **animal dairy becomes more explicitly agricultural and artisanal** — perhaps more expensive, more traceable, more pasture-oriented, more like wine or traditional cheese already is at the high end.

And weirdly, that could actually restore some dignity to animal agriculture. If the industrial necessity disappears, keeping animals becomes less about maximizing output at minimal cost and more about maintaining a particular relationship with land, animals, and food.

Your intuition about veganism also connects to this. It doesn't have to become culturally dominant to exert influence. A minority practice can function almost like a **persistent moral question embedded in the culture**. Every time a menu says:

> vegetarian  
> vegan  
> dairy-free

it quietly reminds everyone that eating animals is, in fact, a choice rather than simply the definition of food.

A century ago, in many contexts, that choice might barely have been cognitively present. Now it routinely is.

You don't need everyone to become vegan for that to matter. It changes what restaurants develop, what supermarkets stock, what food scientists work on, what investors fund, and eventually what becomes ordinary.

And I think your last position is actually a pretty workable human one:

**“I'm probably going to keep eating cheese. I'm also willing to look.”**

Those don't have to resolve immediately into either purity or indifference.

There is a huge territory between _“I must eliminate every ethically compromised product from my life”_ and _“I refuse to know where anything comes from.”_

You can know a little more. Prefer something better when the distinction is meaningful and accessible. Support technologies and farming practices that reduce suffering. Occasionally pay more where you care enough to do so. And still eat the block of feta already sitting in your refrigerator without turning lunch into a referendum on your moral worth.

And your final formulation matters: **reducing unnecessary suffering and improving animal welfare is a value you regard as intrinsically good.** Once that's present, you don't necessarily need to force yourself into a complete ideology. You can let that value exert pressure on your choices as better choices actually become available.

So yes, “good people are working on it” is not a bad place to leave the feta. There is something reassuring about discovering that the uncomfortable question you just stumbled into is already somebody else's life's work.
